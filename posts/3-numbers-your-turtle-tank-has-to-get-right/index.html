3 numbers your turtle tank has to get right

Water at 76-82 °F, a basking spot of 90-95 °F, and a UVB bulb that is replaced on a schedule. The three numbers an aquatic turtle tank lives or dies by, why the UVB one is the one new keepers skip, and how a cheap sensor fits around them.

A turtle tank can look perfect and still be wrong. The water is clear, the dock floats, the turtle swims around, and nothing on the outside tells you that the water is too cold to digest food or that the light over the dock has stopped doing its job months ago.

Before any electronics go near a tank, three numbers have to be right: the water temperature, the basking temperature, and UVB. No sensor fixes a tank that is set up wrong. This post walks through all three, the mistake that quietly breaks the third one, and where a water sensor actually helps.

One caveat first, straight from the book: this is general guidance for aquatic turtles such as sliders. Your species may want different numbers, so check a care sheet written for it, and ask a reptile vet if anything looks off.

Number 1: the water, 76 to 82 °F

Sliders want water in the mid to high 70s Fahrenheit, with hatchlings at the warmer end. The band the book treats as "fine" is 76 to 82 °F (about 24.5 to 28 °C).

Outside that band, things go wrong in two directions:

  • Below the low 70s, a turtle gets sluggish and stops eating.
  • Above 85 °F, he is being cooked slowly.

The tool for this is a submersible aquarium heater with its own thermostat. Add a cheap stick-on thermometer as a second opinion, because it tells you whether the heater is lying.

The trap: a heater that fails on

A heater that fails off is a slow problem you notice in a day. A heater that fails on is an emergency. That difference is the whole reason the water sensor in the book exists: temperature is the one reading from the build that is a real alarm.

The alert bands the book uses look like this:

Water temperatureState
76–82 °FFine
73–76 °F or 82–85 °FWarning, after two bad readings in a row
Under 73 °F or over 85 °FAlert, at once

Why bands and not a single number? A turtle does not care about 79.4 versus 80.1 °F, and a sensor that pages you every time the heater cycles is a sensor you switch off. The bands turn a wobbling line into three states, and the two-readings rule keeps one odd reading from becoming a notification.

If you run Home Assistant instead, the book describes the same alert as an ordinary automation: a numeric state trigger below 73 or above 85 °F, with a for: of 10 minutes so one odd reading does not page you, sending a notification to your phone. If the heater is on a smart plug, the same automation can turn it off above 85 °F.

Number 2: the basking spot, 90 to 95 °F

An aquatic turtle has to get fully out of the water and dry off under a warm light every day. The basking spot should be around 90 to 95 °F at the surface of the dock, with the air above the water in the low 80s. That is the heat bulb's job.

Layout matters here. Heat and light go at one end of the tank so the turtle can choose between warm-and-bright and cool-and-dim, which is how he regulates his own temperature. The heater and filter go at the other end, where the filter's current spreads the heater's warmth.

Note that the basking number is separate from the water number. It lives on the dock, not in the water, and the water sensor does not measure it.

Watch out: keep any camera and electronics box out of the basking lamp's heat and direct beam. The lamp can hit 90 °F plus at a few inches, and bright light straight into a turbidity probe makes its reading drift.

Number 3: UVB, 10 to 12 hours a day

The second bulb is UVB, and it is the one new keepers skip because the tank looks fine without it.

It is not fine. Without UVB a turtle cannot make vitamin D3, cannot use the calcium in his food, and over months his shell softens and deforms.

What the book recommends:

  • Use a reptile UVB bulb. The 5.0 and 10.0 ratings are the common ones for turtles.
  • Mount it so nothing is between the bulb and the turtle. Glass and most plastics block UVB.
  • Run both lights on a timer, 10 to 12 hours a day.
  • Write the UVB bulb's install date on it with a marker.
  • Replace it every 6 to 12 months depending on the bulb.

The trap: a bulb that still glows

UVB bulbs keep glowing long after they stop producing UVB. A bulb that looks fine can be doing nothing, which is why it gets replaced on a schedule, not when it burns out. The marker on the bulb is the schedule.

The other common mistake is mounting the UVB bulb above a glass or acrylic lid. The lid blocks most of the UVB, so the bulb is doing nothing. Mount it over an open section, at the distance the bulb's own packaging gives.

What a sensor can and cannot tell you

The book's water sensor uses three cheap probes, and it is honest about their limits:

ProbeWhat it tells you
DS18B20 (±0.5 °C)Whether the heater is doing its job; the real alarm
TDSHow much has built up since the last water change. A trend meter, not an ammonia test
TurbidityCloudiness: a clogged filter, a bacterial bloom, a messy feeding. Also a trend meter

UVB is not sensed at all; it is a note on the bulb. The basking surface is not sensed either. And a liquid test kit is still the real instrument for water safety: ammonia 0, nitrite 0, nitrate under about 40 ppm. The sensor answers "is the water warm enough, is it time for a water change, did something go cloudy". The test kit answers "is the water safe". Both together is the setup.

Checklist

  • Water held at 76–82 °F by a thermostatic submersible heater, with a stick-on thermometer as a cross-check.
  • An alert for under 73 °F or over 85 °F, and warnings in the bands next to those.
  • A basking dock the turtle can fully climb out on, 90–95 °F at its surface, air above the water in the low 80s.
  • Heat and light at one end, heater and filter at the other.
  • A reptile UVB bulb (5.0 or 10.0) with nothing between it and the turtle, no glass or acrylic lid in the way.
  • Both lights on a timer, 10–12 hours a day.
  • Install date written on the UVB bulb; replaced every 6–12 months, not when it dies.
  • Electronics and probes kept out of the basking lamp's heat and beam.
  • A liquid test kit for ammonia, nitrite and nitrate, because no cheap probe replaces it.
